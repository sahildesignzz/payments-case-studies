Case study 3 · Revolut · grade B− (outside team tested the live app)

Paying by asking: Revolut’s AI assistant

An AI assistant makes paying easier to find, not faster. It understands what you want, then stumbles on the payment details: who, from where, and actually doing it.

★ The conclusive finding · p42

8.1 Buying an eSIM: AIR vs the traditional flow

Traditional Revolut flow: ~45 seconds; 6 taps Open eSIM section → Select destination → Choose plan → Review details → Confirm purchase → Complete payment

AIR flow: ~50 seconds; 6 taps including typing Open Account → Open Chats → Open AIR → Type request → Wait for processing → Select plan → Confirm purchase

AIR created a more guided experience, but not a faster one. Typing the request, waiting for processing, and reviewing AIR's generated response added enough time to make the standard flow slightly quicker.

AIR becomes useful when users do not know where to go, what feature to use, or which filter to apply. Its strength is reducing cognitive effort, not always reducing time.

In one line: the AI assistant doesn't make paying faster. It makes paying easier to find.

  • Method (p42): the same task was done twice, first through AIR and then through the normal interface. "The goal was not to create a formal benchmark."
  • Pros and cons listed on the page: more guided; useful if you don't know where eSIM lives; not faster for people who know the app; typing and processing eat the time saved.

Four screens, four findings

1Understands the intent, can’t pay the recipient p35
  • Highlighted:

    Payments and transfers exposed AIR's biggest gap. The assistant often understood what the user wanted, but failed to identify the right account, recipient, Pocket, or destination. This created a clear disconnect between intent recognition and execution.

  • Evidence on the page (7.1, P2P payments and finding contacts):
    • AIR couldn't find a personal HSBC account that appeared regularly in the user's history.
    • It couldn't find a tennis coach the user had paid before, even though the coach had a Revolut account.
    • With contact access switched on, it found only one more contact and still missed the expected recipients.
    • "AIR could search merchant transactions well, but struggled with people and payment destinations."
2Finds the bill, won’t start the split p37
  • Highlighted:

    AIR understood the bill-splitting request and identified the relevant restaurant transaction. But it did not start the split bill flow. Instead, it gave step-by-step instructions for completing the process manually. This repeated a pattern seen in other areas: AIR is good at finding context, but less consistent at executing actions.

  • Pros: finds the right transaction; understands the intent; gives instructions that fit the situation.
  • Cons: doesn't run the split itself; leans on the existing Revolut screens.
3Keeps an unresolved stolen-card risk in view p21
  • Highlighted:

    This was a strong UX signal. AIR did not simply retain chat history. It appeared to prioritize unresolved security risks and keep them visible until the user addressed them.

  • Context: AIR kept remembering a card reported as possibly stolen, even after the chat moved on to subscriptions and travel. "AIR's persistence reduced the risk of a critical security issue being forgotten."
  • Pros: strong memory across unrelated topics; security put ahead of convenience; feels proactive.
  • Cons: repeated reminders could become intrusive; it needs a way to dismiss or snooze security prompts.
4Language sticks after the user switches p38
  • Highlighted:

    AIR switched to Ukrainian naturally when the conversation changed language. However, it stayed in Ukrainian until the user explicitly asked it to switch back to English.

  • Context: "AIR treats language as persistent conversational context, not a temporary one-message adjustment."
  • Cons: the language can stick longer than expected, and the user has to tell it to switch back.
5What AIR is for: finding, not speed p8
  • Highlighted and underlined:

    Its main value here is not speed. It is reducing the effort needed to find and interpret financial data.

  • Highlighted:

    Important note: AIR does not create a new chat for each scenario. Every question remains in the same persistent thread, even across days or unrelated topics.

  • Context, not highlighted: AIR is reached via Home → Account → Chats → AIR. It uses the usual assistant pattern: suggested prompts, chat input, voice, attachments, one continuous conversation.
6Final findings p57

AIR is strongest as a discovery layer. It helps users find features, understand financial data, and navigate tasks that would otherwise require searching through the app.

Speed is not the main advantage yet. For simple tasks, manual navigation can still be faster. AIR's value is more about guidance, context, and reducing cognitive effort.

Trust depends on reliability. When AIR recognizes transactions, accounts, or payees correctly, the experience feels powerful. When it fails, users need clear signals and easy ways to verify results.

Security behaviour is one of AIR's strongest UX signals. The assistant remembers sensitive context, avoids exposing protected data, and guides users toward secure in-app flows.

AI works best when it supports the app, not replaces it. AIR adds a new interaction layer on top of the existing product. The strongest experience happens when conversation, interface, confirmation, and security work together.

Read together, the highlights make one argument about payments as a feature:

  1. Understanding ≠ executing. AIR gets the intent (pay my coach, split this bill) but fails at the payment-specific parts: who gets paid and from where (p35), and actually starting the flow (p37).
  2. Easier, not faster. Its value is less thinking, not less time (p8, p42 ★, p57).
  3. Memory is double-edged. Remembering an unresolved security risk is its best behaviour (p21). The same persistence makes language stick when it shouldn't (p38) and keeps one endless thread (p8).
  4. Trust is earned on the payment details. Getting the recipient or account right is what makes it feel powerful, and getting it wrong is what breaks trust (p35, p57).
