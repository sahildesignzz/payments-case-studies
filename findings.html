Findings: how design solved and improved payments as a feature

Round 1 · gathered 29 Oct 2026 · apps: Wise, Revolut, Cash App, Apple Pay

This note collects what existing, published case studies say about how design made payments work better. Every point names its source and how strong its evidence is. Nothing here is our own opinion unless it is marked our note.

How to read the evidence grade

GradeMeansExample
AMeasured result from a controlled test (A/B or holdback) by the company or platformStripe holdback test: Apple Pay +22.3% conversion
BA designer or team inside the company reports a number, but there is no method, sample size or significanceWise designer: new calculator gave a 10% conversion uplift
CExpert or agency analysis with no data, i.e. reasoned opinion925 Studios: "tracking UI reduces support load"
DDated, self-reported or unverifiable claim. Kept only as context.Instacart 2016: "58% faster with Apple Pay"

Most of what is published sits at C. The A-grade material is mostly about Apple Pay, because Stripe publishes test data. The deepest independent benchmarking (Built for Mars, Baymard Premium) is paywalled. See the "Locked" section.

1. Wise: international money transfer

Core feature: send money across currencies. The calculator (amount in, currencies, fee, rate, amount received) is the entry point to the whole transfer.

1.1 Redesigning the calculator and the components under it B

  • Source: Ness Grixti, Design Lead, Wise Calculator Redesign (portfolio case study, ©2025). https://nessgrixti.com/portfolio/wise-calculator/
  • Team: Design Lead plus Staff, Senior and Graduate designers, a Tech Lead, and iOS, Android and Web engineers, working with Wise's Send team.
  • Problem (stated):
    • The calculator "has powered cross-currency transfers for over a decade" and drives 87% of volume, plus much of the revenue and profit.
    • It had barely changed and "remained a weak point for conversion".
    • Fixing it showed that the foundational components (buttons, list items, avatars) were weak, so the fix had to happen in the design system, not just on one screen.
  • Research: a component audit to find where changes would help the most products. No user-research methods are described.
  • How design solved it, built in "atomic" phases from small to large:
    1. Roundels (avatar, status, icon button)
      • Sizes were inconsistent → one consistent sizing.
      • There was no standard for paired avatars (e.g. you → recipient) → paired avatars with safe zones.
      • Tappable and non-tappable roundels were both flat grey, so users couldn't tell what was tappable → separate interactive and static variants.
    2. Buttons
      • Two button types did the same job in different styles → one button, three sizes, several styles.
      • Sizes and accessories were missing → avatar and icon support.
      • Disabled buttons confused users → a "button prompt" extension that tells the user what to do next instead of leaving a dead grey button. our note This is a direct functional fix: the user is never stuck without knowing why.
    3. List item
      • 7 near-identical components across 100k+ Figma instances → one flexible list item.
      • Clickable and static rows looked the same → accessories that signal interactivity.
      • Some rows needed emphasis → "spotlight" support.
    4. Money input: the Send team's new input was "lifted and shifted" back into the design system, so 4 core money-movement flows use the same input. Light usage guidance was added for support teams.
  • Results (self-reported, no method): +10% conversion from the new calculator, 10+ teams helped, 4 core money-movement flows supported.
  • Missing from the source: fee/rate display decisions, error states, accessibility, localisation, dates.
  • Why it matters for us: this is the clearest case of design-system work turning into a payment-conversion result.

1.2 Showing the full cost before the user commits C

  • Sources:
    • 925 Studios, Wise App Design Breakdown (agency blog, reviewed by Yusuf, Lead Designer; dated Apr/Oct 2026). https://www.925studios.co/blog/wise-design-breakdown-fintech-transparency
    • Raw Studio, Philippe Hong, How Wise Uses 3 Transparency-First UX Principles (18 Jul 2026). https://raw.studio/blog/how-wise-uses-3-transparency-first-ux-principles/
    • Built for Mars company page (free summary). https://builtformars.com/company/wise
  • Problem: international transfers hide cost. A low fee can be offset by a poor exchange rate, and fees appear at different stages.
  • Design solution:
    • The calculator sits on the homepage, before sign-up. Built for Mars confirms that "a fee calculator is linked from the splash page".
    • The same calculator appears in the app and on the recipient confirmation screen.
    • It shows amount sent, fee, exchange rate and amount the recipient gets, all before payment details are entered.
  • How it helps the function: the user can judge the result while the decision can still be undone, without doing the maths or filling in forms first (Raw Studio). 925 Studios says it also pre-qualifies users, so the people who continue have already accepted the price.
  • Quote (Raw Studio): "Trust should be earned before commitment, not requested at the moment of commitment."
  • Evidence: none specific to Wise. 925 Studios cites a cross-industry survey (FTA × Morning Consult, June 2025): 67% of consumers say clear pricing is the top trust driver, against 55% for user-friendly design and 52% for real-time visibility. This is context, not proof about Wise.

1.3 Breaking down how the exchange rate is calculated C

  • Source: 925 Studios, Raw Studio (above).
  • Design solution:
    • The mid-market rate is shown with a link to its source (Reuters).
    • The fee is a separate labelled line, not hidden inside the rate.
    • The rate is placed inside the calculation, linked directly to the amount received, not in fine print.
  • How it helps: any markup is visible in the layout itself, and the user can check how the money sent becomes the money received.
  • Quote (Raw Studio): "The user is not only shown the result. They are shown how the result was produced."
  • Related (Built for Mars free summary): once your savings pass a threshold, Wise compares its fees against banks and other providers.

1.4 Signing up and verifying identity in stages C

  • Source: 925 Studios.
  • Problem: identity verification (KYC) is the step with the most friction in onboarding.
  • Design solution: the user creates an account, sets up a transfer and sees the fee and delivery estimate first, and only then starts KYC. KYC is split into labelled steps ("Step 2 of 4") with one input or upload per screen.
  • How it helps: the pending transfer gives the user a reason to finish verification.
  • Quote: the aim is "to delay it until value has already been delivered."
  • Evidence: none for Wise. 925 Studios' own "60% onboarding drop-off" figure has no source. Treat it as invented.

1.5 Tracking the transfer after sending C

  • Sources: 925 Studios, Raw Studio.
  • Problem: transfers can take 1–5 business days. The money moves through banks and compliance checks the user can't see, which causes anxiety, repeated app checks and support contacts.
  • Design solution: a tracker with stages: initiated → processing → sent to bank → received. Each stage has a timestamp and a plain-language description of what has happened, what is happening now and what comes next.
  • How it helps: the user stays in control while waiting. Both sources claim it reduces support load, but neither measures it.
  • Quote (925 Studios): "Wise's tracking UI is a support cost reduction mechanism dressed as a customer experience feature."

1.6 Smaller design moves that support the payment task C

  • Four-item navigation (Send, Account, Cards, More) keeps the focus on finishing tasks (925 Studios).
  • Visual hierarchy for money: green for primary actions, grey for secondary information, and numbers always larger than their labels (925 Studios).
  • Invoice upload: upload a PDF invoice and the recipient details are filled in automatically. This removes a lot of manual entry and error risk (Built for Mars free summary).
  • Weak spots 925 Studios names:
    • With three or more currencies, the multi-currency Account screen doesn't prioritise anything.
    • New users mix up "Send" (a transfer) with "Account" (holding currencies).
    • Notification and privacy settings are buried.

1.7 Non-design context

  • Checkout.com case study: Wise moved to Checkout.com's Payouts and Unified Payments API, and approval rates rose 1%. This is infrastructure, not design, so it doesn't count as a design solution. https://www.casestudies.com/company/checkoutcom/case-study/best-in-class-payment-performance-boosts-the-bottom-line-for-wise
  • UserTesting case study: Wise built a 12-person research team running up to 30 remote tests a week (from 2018). It tells us how Wise works, but not which payment screens changed. https://www.casestudies.com/company/usertesting/case-study/how-wise-relies-on-usertesting-to-help-over-10-million-people-and-businesses-manage-their-money-across-the-world

2. Revolut: app-wide money (send, exchange, cards, subscriptions)

2.1 Payroll onboarding: removing a forced dependency B

  • Source: Max Karacun, Product Designer, Tackling Low Onboarding Conversion Rate (Revolut Business Payroll; 2-week project; undated). https://maxkaracun.com/revolut-case-study
  • Team: product designer, product manager, 2 back-end engineers, 2 front-end engineers, a payroll specialist.
  • Goal: raise onboarding funnel conversion from 15.6% to 25.0%.
  • Problem: a heavy drop-off at the "add people" step, where the admin adds employees who will be paid.
  • Research: an existing in-product feedback survey plus conversations with payroll clients.
    • Reason 1: it was hard to add an employee.
    • Reason 2: admins didn't want to force employees to open Revolut accounts just to be paid.
  • Hypothesis tested and rejected: "users lack permissions". Data showed 96% of users who completed onboarding had them. our note A good example of checking a guess against data before designing.
  • Benchmark: every competitor had an employee step, and none required employees to register on their platform.
  • Design solution:
    • Discoverability: made "Add people" more prominent using the design system's header patterns.
    • "Decoupling users": inviting employees to Revolut became optional, not mandatory.
    • Three options compared:
      • A, minimal change: an optional invite when adding someone. Cheap to build, but confusing when importing a large FPS file.
      • B, chosen: start with an empty list and add new employees (optional invite) or existing Revolut-linked ones. Medium effort. Handles FPS (bank payment file) imports with many non-Revolut staff and can be reused in the payroll product. Downsides: slightly slower for existing team members, and a risk of duplicates.
      • C, rejected: merge the manual and FPS-import flows. Too complex.
    • Went straight to high fidelity because the design system was already in place.
  • Result: drop-off at the "add people" step fell from 61.5% to 34.5%. No sample size, time period or significance is given.
  • Limits it admits: conversion is still below the typical 30–50% range; Option B risks duplicate records; work is ongoing.
  • Why it matters: a payments-adjacent B2B flow where the functional fix was a rule change (decoupling), which design brought to light and shaped.

2.2 The "delight" layer around payments C

  • Source: Nesrine Changuel, The Revolut Delight Case Study (Product Delight Tips, Substack, 29 Jul 2025). https://nesrinechanguel.substack.com/p/the-revolut-delight-case-study-1c3
  • Exchange and holding several currencies: hold, swap and spend currencies at real-time rates with transparent pricing. This removes the problems of poor airport rates and juggling cash.
  • Sending money: international transfers using only a phone number, with no SWIFT-style bank codes to enter.
  • Card security (fixing legitimate payments being wrongly blocked abroad):
    • Location-based alerts: a purchase in one country while the phone is in another is flagged immediately.
    • Freeze and unfreeze in one tap.
    • Disposable virtual cards for online shopping, with a new number after each use.
  • Subscriptions: a tracker for recurring payments and cancellation inside the app.
  • Notifications: spending categories, and a "welcome home" message after a trip.
  • Evidence: none. This is product-delight analysis, and the "effect" is the author's view.

2.4 The Revolut AIR assistant: paying through conversation B−, studied by Sahil

  • Source: Craft Innovations, Inside Revolut AIR: Complete UX Breakdown (the paid PDF, which Sahil has). Full highlights are in notes/revolut-air-highlights.md.
  • ★ Conclusive finding (p42): buying an eSIM took ~45 s / 6 taps through the normal flow and ~50 s / 6 taps through AIR. AIR is "more guided … but not a faster one". "Its strength is reducing cognitive effort, not always reducing time." This was a single informal run.
  • Biggest payments gap (p35): AIR understands the intent but fails to pick the right account, recipient, Pocket or destination. It missed an HSBC account and a tennis coach it had paid before.
  • Bill splitting (p37): it finds the right transaction but doesn't start the split. It gives manual instructions instead.
  • Security memory (p21): it keeps an unresolved stolen-card risk visible across unrelated topics.
  • Final findings (p57): a discovery layer, not a speed layer; trust depends on getting payees right; AI should support the app, not replace it.

2.5 Rejected for Revolut

  • RivalSense: "+42% engagement after the virtual-card redesign". No source, so excluded.
  • Behance and Medium redesign concepts (e.g. "detect nearby payee"): concepts with no results.

3. Cash App: peer-to-peer payments

Honest status: the weakest-covered app. I found no published case study by Cash App's own team.

3.1 Learning curve versus speed C, mostly locked

  • Source: Peter Ramsey, Built for Mars, Why CashApp Has A Steep Learning Curve (2 Dec 2021, edited 1 Apr 2025, BFM+). https://builtformars.com/case-studies/signing-up-to-cash-app
  • Argument (free intro): something "easy to do" isn't the same as something that "feels intuitive" to a new user. Cash App is fast for experts, like a pilot's cockpit, but "a complicated mess" for everyone else.
  • Section titles: Aim for a soft landing, How not to give away $100, Deciding what you'll have for dinner is hard, Breaking the social contract. The body is paywalled, so I can't report what they argue.
  • An earlier search summary said the app asks for a phone number immediately with no welcome screen, and that cutting clicks too far can hurt the experience. This comes from the search snippet, not a fetched page, so treat it as unverified.

3.2 Product decisions reported in the press D, news, not UX analysis

  • Payment links (PYMNTS): shareable request links people can send by text or social apps. A Cash App product lead said in-app requests "could feel overly formal", so links let people use their own tone. https://www.pymnts.com/?p=3473207
  • Instalments on P2P (PYMNTS): split P2P transfers of $25+ into short-term instalments. https://www.pymnts.com/?p=3619025
  • Amount first: the home screen is an amount keypad; you type the amount, then choose Request or Pay (DesignRush review, third-party).
  • One input per screen during sign-up, e.g. phone, email, $cashtag (gluestack blog, secondary).
  • our note These are the kinds of decisions we'd want to show on a page, but no source backs them with research yet.

4. Apple Pay: checkout (the strongest evidence)

4.1 Adding Apple Pay raises conversion A

  • Stripe holdback experiment across 50+ payment methods (Sen Zhuang and Parker Zhao, Stripe Data Science, 10 Apr 2025). https://stripe.com/blog/testing-the-conversion-impact-of-50-plus-global-payment-methods
    • Apple Pay: +22.3% conversion, +22.5% revenue for global businesses that offered it.
    • For comparison: WeChat Pay +13% / +14%; Revolut Pay +3% conversion / +13% revenue.
    • Reported as statistically significant. Sample size, duration and split are not published.
  • Stripe Sessions 2024 talk, A look under Stripe's hood. https://stripe.com/sessions/2024/a-look-under-stripes-hood-using-data-to-demystify-payments
    • Théo Blochet (Stripe PM): an A/B test adding Apple Pay gave a "10%, statistically-significant conversion uplift".
    • Peek (merchant): Apple Pay +7.8% conversion and 15% faster conversion.
    • Wallets and Link: a 3× faster median checkout than other payment methods.
    • Link among repeat customers: +14% returning-user conversion.
  • Why the numbers differ: different tests, populations and years. Quote each one with its own source, never as a single "Apple Pay lifts X%".

4.2 Placement: show it early, not at the end A/B

  • Stripe (same blog): showing Apple Pay through the Express Checkout Element, early in the flow, averaged a 2× conversion rate compared with showing it at the end of checkout. The "separate Stripe studies" behind this aren't described.
  • Stripe talk: show wallet buttons "before or at the time of checkout". The checkout should sit where the buyer is engaging; "the highest-converting funnel is actually no funnel at all" (Brian McMullin, SamCart).
  • Avoid too many options: don't make a checkout that looks like "the Cheesecake Factory menu" (McMullin).
  • Dynamic display: show the right wallet to the right buyer based on location, amount, browser and currency (Stripe).

4.3 Making Apple Pay the default B

  • Stripe Apple Pay best-practices doc: Wish ran an A/B test on new users who had Apple Pay set up. Defaulting to Apple Pay gave a "2X higher conversion rate increase". The wording is unclear (2× the rate, or a 200% increase?). https://edge-docs.stripe.com/apple-pay/best-practices
  • Same doc, with no evidence given:
    • Show the button on product pages, product lists and search results.
    • Never show a disabled button. If options are missing, point to them after the tap.
    • Remove forced sign-up.
    • Don't ask for data Apple Pay already supplies.
    • Ask for a password after payment, on the confirmation page.
  • Rejected: Indiegogo "+250% conversion" from express checkout. No method, and it's marketing copy.

4.4 Apple's own design rules (first-party HIG: design guidance, not measured results)

Source: Apple Human Interface Guidelines, Apple Pay. https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/apple-pay

  • Offering
    • Offer it on every device and browser that supports it, and hide it where it isn't supported.
    • If you check that a card is in Wallet, Apple Pay must be the primary option, e.g. pre-selected. Don't put it in a separate step.
  • The button
    • Use the Apple Pay button only to pay or to set up Apple Pay.
    • Never hide it or make it look unavailable. If size or colour is missing, explain after the tap.
  • Streamlining
    • "If Apple Pay is available, assume people want to use it": put it first, larger, and separated by a line from the other options.
    • Buy buttons on product pages for single items.
    • Express checkout for the cart.
    • Coupons inside the payment sheet.
    • Collect size, colour and optional data (gift notes) before the sheet.
    • Collect multiple shipping addresses before the sheet.
    • Choose the store pickup location before the sheet.
  • Data: prefer Apple Pay's contact, shipping and payment details over stored ones, which reduces corrections.
  • Accounts: don't require account creation before purchase. Offer it on the confirmation page, pre-filled.
  • Errors: report results in the payment sheet, with errors the user can act on (e.g. a bad address).
  • Confirmation: a thank-you page with shipping timing and how to check status ("1234 (Apple Pay)").
  • Payment sheet: only essential information (e.g. no shipping address for a digital gift card, which would wrongly suggest something physical is coming). Line items for discounts, pending costs, donations, and recurring or future payments with their frequency.

4.5 Real-world friction D, dated

  • 2015, Phoenix Marketing (n = 3,002): two-thirds of Apple Pay users had problems at checkout (slow merchants, cashiers who didn't know it, broken terminals), and 47% found "Apple Pay merchant" stores weren't actually ready. It shows that functionality depends on the whole system, not just the UI. Too old to benchmark with.
  • 2016, Instacart via Quartz: checkout 58% faster with Apple Pay. Self-reported and old.

4.6 Baymard (benchmark, locked)

  • Baymard reviewed 55 Apple page designs. The payment step page was captured 16 Oct 2025 on iPhone X and has 12 highlights of what Apple does right and wrong, all Premium-only. https://baymard.com/checkout-usability/benchmark/step-type/payment/26335-apple
  • This is the presentation format you liked: one page with numbered points and the research behind each. The content itself is locked.
